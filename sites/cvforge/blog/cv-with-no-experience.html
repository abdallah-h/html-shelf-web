How to Write a CV With No Experience (Students & Fresh Graduates)

3 min read · Updated 2026-10-01

Every professional started with an empty CV. Employers who hire graduates and entry-level candidates know this — they are not expecting years of experience. What they want to see is evidence that you can learn, deliver and work with people. The good news: you almost certainly have that evidence already. You just need to present it well.

1. Lead with a short, specific profile

Open with two or three sentences that say who you are, what you're good at and what role you want. Avoid generic phrases that could describe anyone.

✗ "Hard-working and motivated graduate looking for an opportunity to grow."

✓ "Computer Science graduate with hands-on experience building web applications in JavaScript and SQL through university and personal projects. Looking for a junior developer role where I can contribute to front-end features from day one."

2. Put education near the top

When you have little work history, your education is your strongest section, so move it up — right after your profile. Go beyond the degree name:

  • Your grade or GPA, if it's good
  • Your graduation project — what it does and the technologies or methods you used
  • Relevant modules or courses connected to the job
  • Awards, scholarships or competitions

3. Treat projects as experience

University assignments, personal projects, freelance work and open-source contributions all show real skills. Give your best two or three projects their own section and describe them exactly as you would a job: what you built, how, and what the result was.

Online Bookstore — university team project

• Built a web store with search, cart and admin dashboard using ASP.NET Core and SQL Server.
• Designed the database schema and wrote the order-management module.
• Presented the project to a panel and received the highest grade in the course.

4. Include internships, volunteering and part-time jobs

A summer internship, a volunteer role or weekends working in a shop all count. Even if the work isn't related to your target job, it proves reliability and teaches transferable skills: communication, customer service, teamwork, handling pressure and time management. Describe what you achieved, not just what you were assigned.

5. Make your skills section specific

"Computer skills" tells an employer nothing. "Excel (pivot tables, VLOOKUP), Python, Git, Figma" tells them exactly what you can do. Group technical skills into categories and only list tools you could actually discuss in an interview. Add languages with an honest level.

6. Keep it to one page

An entry-level CV should fit on a single page. Use a clean, readable layout, consistent dates and no spelling mistakes — for a graduate, attention to detail is itself a skill being tested.

A simple structure that works

  1. Name and contact details (phone, email, city, LinkedIn or GitHub)
  2. Profile — 2 to 3 sentences
  3. Education — degree, grade, graduation project
  4. Projects — your best 2 or 3
  5. Experience — internships, volunteering, part-time work
  6. Skills and certifications
  7. Languages

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Padding with irrelevant detail — your primary school or a long list of hobbies won't help.
  • Using one CV for every application — adjust your profile and project order for each role.
  • Unprofessional email addresses — use a simple firstname.lastname address.
  • Exaggerating — anything on your CV can become an interview question.
